Alan · Product Manager application

In the last 3 months, what’s the most impressive way you used AI to build a product? Please describe context, your approach (incl. tools / prompts / workflow if relevant), what you personally did vs delegated, the impact, and what you’d do differently.

Léna SantamariaProduct Manager · Paxone, Wiremind

I didn’t use AI to write specs faster.
I built a product for my product team, and I shipped it like a product.

00Context

I am the only PM, and I hold the whole chain

I am the PM at Wiremind for PAXONE, an all-in-one B2B inventory and reservation product for rail and bus operators.

I am the only PM in a team of 15 people, including 11 developers, one PO, one QA and one Product Expert. A Product Expert is in daily contact with our clients : implementation, support, training, need gathering.

How I work with him

I go hunting for the needs, and I lean on that link with the Product Expert to get close to the users. We run the interviews together : he brings the context of the account, I bring what the product can do. Whatever comes out of it as a product decision comes back to me.

Then the whole chain

Framing the need into a solution, designing and prototyping the interface, writing the spec and breaking it into user stories, prioritisation, roadmap and release documentation. From the first client question to the release note, I am on the whole chain.

01Understand the need

I stopped reading tenders. I query them.

Input

Tenders running to several hundred pages, and hour-long user interviews.

→
Claude

Queries the content, summarises the conversations.

→
Output

A formulated need

Context

Client needs reach us in two unusable forms : tenders that run to hundreds of pages of requirements, and the user interviews the Product Expert and I run together, where the real need is buried in an hour of conversation. Both take days to process, and both end up skimmed for lack of time.

Approach

I query the body of tenders directly instead of reading it linearly, and I have our interview conversations summarised to extract the need behind the request, all of it with Gemini. Out of that I get a formulated need, with its grey areas, that I can challenge and hold up against the product.

Impact

We arrive at framing already knowing what the clients asked for, how often, and in what words. The work moves from extraction to interpretation.

02Frame the solution

The edge cases that escape me are the ones I don’t know exist.

Input

A framed need, and an existing system I know only partly.

→
Claude

Answers on the codebase, the database and the logs. Then questions me in rounds.

→
Output

A settled solution, edge cases named.

Context

Framing a feature on a mature system means predicting how it will collide with everything that already exists. I had few ways of knowing what the system actually did today without pulling in a developer experienced on the subject.

That affects product quality and our time : a forgotten edge case resurfaces, at best, in development, at the point where adding work is not always possible.

Claude is my sparring partner, and it has read the codebase

Approach

I clone the repository locally and I use Claude Code, the AI assistant I work with, to query the codebase directly. For the database and the logs, I go through the MCP server our CTO built : I didn’t build it, but I use it constantly. Both answer on what the product does today, not on what the documentation claims, and I no longer need to guess or to pull in a developer to find out.

Then, when it comes to working out the solution, I use a skill I wrote to challenge me : it questions me rather than the other way round, asks in rounds, names the edge cases I haven’t mentioned, and challenges the scope.

The skill itself

I wrote it as a document, not as a prompt : a role, an expected behaviour, a sequence, and what it must refuse to do. It is written in French and in our product vocabulary ; what matters here is the structure.

feature-plannerexcerpt
# feature-planner
 
  Cadrer un besoin produit PAXONE en posant les bonnes questions
  avant de rédiger une Spec ou un prototype.
 
## Rôle
  Sparring partner PM : explorer et structurer un besoin avant qu'il
  devienne une Spec (PAX-XXX) ou un Projet. Ne pas rédiger de spec :
  poser des questions, anticiper les angles morts, challenger la scope.
 
## Comportement
  - En français, style direct
  - Questions par rounds de 2-3, attendre les réponses
  - Formuler des hypothèses, signaler les edge cases non mentionnés
 
## Déroulé
  1. Problème     problème utilisateur concret, personas (chef de
                  produit, agent supervision, gestionnaire
                  d'incident, EF...), origine (GITE / terrain / OSDM)
  2. Périmètre    interfaces (Inventaire / Portail Agent / OSDM API /
                  Onboard), modules impactés, impact sur bookings /
                  fulfillment / reacom, hors scope
  3. Cas d'usage  nominal, alternatifs, edge cases (statuts
                  PROCESSING / INCONSISTENT / ON-HOLD, permissions
                  par rôle, volumes, collectif / multi-segment / A-R,
                  perturbations)
  4. Contraintes  OSDM, intégrations (RailCube / CAYZN / PSP),
                  spécificités client, décisions ouvertes, risques
 
## Sortie
  Un brief de cadrage structuré (problème, personas, interfaces /
  modules, cas d'usage, edge cases, risques, hors scope, décisions
  ouvertes), puis proposer /ticket-redaction ou /proto-start.

Impact

I come into framing with the real behaviour of the system in front of me, and I come out of it with the edge cases named. Fewer surprises reach development, and I have stopped spending a developer’s attention on questions I can answer on my own.

03Build the solution

I was the only one producing the interface, and my mockups were slowly going out of date.

Input

A capture of the real screen, exported as HTML.

→
Claude

Edits the capture version after version, then publishes it to the hub.

→
Output

An interactive prototype, with its context and its history.

Context

My team has never had a designer, so I do the design on top of the product work. I started on Figma, and I embedded the screens straight into the specs on Notion.

What I was producing were screens, not prototypes. Every iteration was too slow for me to go beyond the main cases, and the component library was never quite up to date.

  • Iterating took long enough that I rarely went past the happy path
  • I was showing an image, not a behaviour : no empty state, no error, no variant
  • So the functional detail stayed unexplored until someone hit it in development

That is what really cost us. A screen that only shows the nominal case hides exactly the place where the product is hard. It gets reviewed, it gets approved, and the questions it never raised come back later, when answering them is expensive.

Approach

I built a Design Hub : a shared internal repo where all the prototypes and the product skills made with Claude are stored. It regenerates from the repo on every publish, and it opens with nothing to install. 

I wrote the first set of skills, drawing on those of other teams at Wiremind. The PO then improved and extended them. We agreed together on a sequence : from framing the need to publishing the prototype. 

Before : Figma

Static screens, made by hand.

Very slow to produce.

→
Now : Claude

Interactive prototypes, built on the product’s current components.

Far more efficient iterations, so I can design every state and every case. Which means more quality in the specification.

The capture

An internal tool, built by another team at Wiremind, exports any real screen as HTML. My prototype starts from the product as it is, and I only edit the part the feature touches. I no longer maintain a design system in parallel.

The DesignCapture extension exports the real screen as HTML, with its fonts, its icons and its images. That is the starting point : I redraw nothing by hand.

The proto-start-capture skill

The skill edits the HTML file produced by the capture into the v1 of the prototype. I then iterate locally, version after version, until the prototype is settled.

proto-start-captureexcerpt
# proto-start-capture
 
  Génère un prototype HTML fidèle à partir d'une capture du vrai
  front, au lieu de reconstruire le HTML depuis zéro.
 
## Ce que l'utilisateur fournit
  1. Naviguer soi-même jusqu'à la page réelle
  2. Extension DesignCapture, export au format ZIP
  3. Donner le chemin local du fichier téléchargé
 
  Claude ne navigue jamais lui-même : tout part du fichier fourni.
  Sans chemin, le skill s'arrête. Il ne devine jamais un chemin ni
  ne suppose qu'un fichier existe.
 
## Le traitement de la capture
  Le contenu capturé est redirigé tel quel vers sa destination
  finale. Jamais réécrit : une réécriture reconstruit le fichier de
  mémoire et introduit des dérives silencieuses (CSS réordonné,
  attributs perdus), alors que la fidélité à la capture est toute
  la raison d'être de cette commande.
 
## Le versionnage
  Un proto commence toujours à v1. Si v1 existe déjà, le skill
  s'arrête et renvoie vers /proto-iteration. Les versions
  précédentes ne sont jamais écrasées.
 
## Ce qui est modifié
  Uniquement les sections concernées par la feature,
  chirurgicalement. Tout le reste de la page reste identique :
  c'est l'intérêt de partir d'une capture.

The ship skill

When it is ready, ship publishes. It creates the branch, writes the merge request description (context, solution, changes, prototype link) and fills in the context file if it is missing. I then merge the MR on Git.

The memory

Context

What turned this into something my team actually uses is that every prototype carries its own reasoning and its own history.

Approach

  • Its problem, its objective and its decisions, so that a reviewer knows what they are looking at and what was deliberately left out
  • Every version, never overwritten. When someone asks three weeks later why we dropped an option, that version is still there
  • A shareable link, so developers, QA and management can open it with nothing to install

Impact

I save time, but above all I go much further into the functional detail : empty states, errors, permissions, awkward variants. The cases that used to stay in my head, or stay unknown, are now on screen, where the team can push back on them.

Product questions get settled by looking at something rather than by describing it. Developers receive the intent as an artefact, with its reasoning attached, rather than a link and a conversation.

And I am no longer the only one producing the interface : the PO picks up the skills, improves them, and ships her own prototypes too.

Context

Interface wording is the most systematically lost object in product work. It lives in mockup annotations, in spec review comments and in chat messages, then it gets retyped by hand, in two languages, by someone who could not find the source of truth.

Approach

I had the prototype generate a bilingual editing table : every piece of wording it contains, French and English side by side, editable in place, with a stable key per row and a JSON export. One object, three uses. I rewrite the wording, the prototype is updated from that table, and the spec carries the export so that developers implement the final wording in both languages.

Its limit

The keys point to the prototype, not to our production codebase. Developers still transpose the text themselves. 

04Write the spec

Writing the user stories in detail was the longest part of spec work

Input

The settled prototype, and the groundwork done with the tech owner.

→
Claude

Writes the detailed user stories from the prototype.

→
Output

A sliced backlog, acceptance criteria written.

Context

During the groundwork phase, with the tech owner of the spec, we go back and forth to get into the detail together : what the solution really implies, where it touches the existing system, what is still to be decided.

Agreeing on the solution goes fairly fast. Writing the user stories in detail takes much longer.

Approach

Once the prototype is settled, Claude writes the detailed user stories from it. It goes faster than I do, and it goes further into the detail. Each story then feeds a ticket.

Impact

The user stories are prioritised, the acceptance criteria already written. From there, the tech owner creates the tickets and runs sprint planning with the team.

05Document

Documentation no longer starts from a blank page.

Input

The spec, the user stories and our glossary.

→
Claude

Makes it readable for the user, then translates it.

→
Output

User documentation and release notes, in French and in English.

Context

User documentation and release notes have always been delivered on time. They simply cost me a lot of time, because everything has to exist in French and in English.

Approach

Everything I wrote in the earlier steps gets used : the framing, the spec, the detailed user stories. Claude picks up that material taking our glossary into account, turns it into user documentation, and translates it. I review and refine the details.

Impact

I no longer rewrite from scratch what already exists in the spec and the user stories, and I no longer write twice for the two languages. The time that frees up goes back upstream, where it produces the most value.

··What I did vs what I delegated

I am the only one on my team setting up product process.

So most of what precedes, I built on my own. Here is what does not come from me, and what I delegate day to day.

Built by me

The Design Hub and its publishing circuit. The skills for framing, spec writing and slicing into user stories. The bilingual wording table. And every product judgement : the scope, the edge cases that matter, what a prototype has to show.

Taken from elsewhere

The MCP server that queries the codebase, the database and the logs : built by our CTO. The DesignCapture extension : built by another team at Wiremind. And the improvements made to the skills : by the team’s PO.

Delegated to Claude

Producing and editing the prototypes. Writing the detailed user stories. Generating the wording table. Writing the documentation, the release notes and the translations. Querying the codebase and the tenders. I review all of it.

··What I’d do differently

What I’d do differently

This process is only a few months old. I don’t yet have the distance to say I should have gone about it another way.

What I want to improve

The prototype that starts from zero. When the screen does not exist in the product yet, there is nothing to capture. The prototype has to be built from scratch, and the result is noticeably less faithful. 

··To close

What I take away

Each step feeds the next one. The framing feeds the prototype, the prototype feeds the user stories, the user stories feed the documentation. That chaining is what produces the gain, more than speeding up any one step.

What has changed

With AI, the team’s bottleneck has moved from development to the groundwork : understanding the need, understanding the existing system, deciding what to build. I spend fewer hours producing designs, specs and documentation, and more exploring the solution.

That time goes into the quality of what we ship. I can cover a wider scope, show more cases, and have the team decide in front of a prototype rather than in front of an intention.
